Module 1 · Basic Numeracy & Calculation Skills

1.1 Number Systems: Whole, Integer, Rational & Real

Number systems are nested inside one another, like Russian nesting dolls: every whole number is an integer, every integer is a rational number, and every rational number is a real number.

Whole Numbers

0, 1, 2, 3, 4, ... — no fractions, no decimals, and never negative.

Integers

Whole numbers plus their negative counterparts: ..., -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, ... Zero is an integer but is neither positive nor negative.

Rational Numbers

Any number that can be written as a fraction a/b (b ≠ 0). This includes all integers (4 = 4/1) and decimals that terminate or repeat forever (e.g., 0.25 or 0.3333...).

Real Numbers

Every number that can be placed on a number line — all rational numbers plus irrational numbers like π or √2, whose decimals never end and never repeat.

Whole Numbers ⊂ Integers ⊂ Rational Numbers ⊂ Real Numbers
Figure 1.1: Each number system is fully contained within the next larger one.

1.2 Ordering Numbers, Intervals & Set Notation

On a number line, values increase from left to right. For negative numbers, the value farther from zero is smaller (e.g., -5 < -2). Decimals and fractions are placed between the two whole numbers they fall between (4.3 sits between 4 and 5, not 4 and 3).

Inequality Symbols

< means "less than" and > means "greater than." The open side of the symbol always points toward the larger quantity. ≤ and ≥ include the boundary value itself (less/greater than or equal to).

Discrete vs. Continuous Sets

A discrete set contains distinct, separate, unconnected values (e.g., whole numbers, or the number of cars someone owns). A continuous set has no gaps — any value in the interval is possible (e.g., real numbers, age, temperature).

Interval Notation

A parenthesis "(" or ")" excludes the boundary value (matches < or >). A bracket "[" or "]" includes the boundary value (matches ≤ or ≥). Infinity, ∞, is always paired with a parenthesis since it isn't a value that can be "reached."

1 < x < 2 → (1, 2)
1 ≤ x ≤ 2 → [1, 2]
4 < x ≤ 9 → (4, 9]
x > -2 → (-2, ∞)
x ≤ 8 → (-∞, 8]
Discrete set example: integers 5 to 16 inclusive → {5, 6, 7, ..., 16}
Figure 1.2: The interval (-1, 2] on a number line — open at -1, closed at 2.

1.3 Order of Operations (PEMDAS) & Exponents

Exponents

An exponent shows repeated multiplication. In 4³, 4 is the base and 3 is the exponent: 4³ = 4 × 4 × 4 = 64. Any nonzero base raised to the 0 power equals 1; any base raised to the 1st power equals itself. A negative base raised to an even exponent gives a positive result; raised to an odd exponent it stays negative.

4³ = 4 × 4 × 4 = 64
(-2)² = -2 × -2 = 4 (even exponent → positive)
(-2)³ = -2 × -2 × -2 = -8 (odd exponent → negative)
8⁰ = 1    5¹ = 5

Order of Operations — PEMDAS

When an expression has multiple operations, work through them in this order: Parentheses → Exponents (and roots) → Multiplication and Division (left to right, equal priority) → Addition and Subtraction (left to right, equal priority).

8 - 4 ÷ 2 = 8 - 2 = 6 (divide before subtracting)
4 + 9 · 10 = 4 + 90 = 94
8² - 21 = 64 - 21 = 43
4(2² + 26) ÷ 7 = 4(16 + 26) ÷ 7 = 4(42) ÷ 7 = 168 ÷ 7 = 24
Figure 1.3: PEMDAS — work left to right within each tied priority level (M/D together, A/S together).
Tip: Memorize PEMDAS as "Please Excuse My Dear Aunt Sally" — but remember M/D are one tied step (left to right) and so are A/S. Working left to right within a tier is the #1 place students lose points.

1.4 Estimation

Estimation approximates a solution using rounded or simplified values instead of exact numbers. It's useful when exact data isn't available, when precision isn't necessary, or to sanity-check a complex calculation.

Three-Point Estimation

Combines a best-case, most-likely, and worst-case estimate into a single weighted value, giving more weight to the most-likely scenario.

E = [best-case + (4 × most-likely) + worst-case] / 6
  • Estimating saves time when exact data is unavailable or unnecessary.
  • Always know whether your estimate is likely to run high or low (e.g., rounding 12 down to 10 underestimates the total).
  • Don't estimate when precision matters (e.g., manufacturing tolerances) or when exact numbers are just as easy to compute.

1.5 Factors, Prime Factorization, GCF & Square Roots

Factors & Divisibility

A factor of a number divides it evenly, with no remainder. For example, 5 and 6 are both factors of 30 because 5 × 6 = 30.

Prime vs. Composite

A prime number has exactly two positive factors: 1 and itself (e.g., 2, 3, 5, 7, 11). A composite number has additional factors beyond 1 and itself (e.g., 10 = 1, 2, 5, 10). Every integer greater than 1 is either prime or composite.

Prime Factorization

The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic states every composite number is a unique product of prime numbers. A factor tree breaks a number down step by step until only primes remain.

30 = 3 × 10 = 3 × 2 × 5  →  Prime factorization of 30 = 2 × 3 × 5

Greatest Common Factor (GCF)

The largest factor two numbers share. Useful for simplifying fractions.

Factors of 12: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12
Factors of 32: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32
GCF(12, 32) = 4

Square Roots

A square root is the number that, multiplied by itself, produces the given number (a perfect square). Every positive real number has a positive (principal) square root and a negative square root. Square roots are treated like exponents in order of operations — evaluate them right after parentheses.

√16 = 4 (since 4² = 16)
-√49 = -7
√25 + 10 = 5 + 10 = 15
Figure 1.5: Factor tree for 30 — branching stops once every leaf is prime. Prime factorization of 30 = 2 × 3 × 5.

1.6 Operating with Negative Numbers

Negative numbers show up constantly in this course — z-scores, residuals, differences between means, and deviations from the mean can all be negative. Here's a quick reference for the sign rules.

Addition

Same signs: add the absolute values and keep the common sign. Different signs: subtract the smaller absolute value from the larger, and keep the sign of the number with the larger absolute value.

(-5) + (-3) = -8   (same sign: add, keep sign)
(-5) + 3 = -2   (different signs: subtract, keep sign of larger absolute value)
5 + (-3) = 2

Subtraction

Subtracting a number is the same as adding its opposite. Rewrite the subtraction as addition, then apply the addition rules above. This is why "minus a negative" becomes a positive.

a - b = a + (-b)
5 - (-3) = 5 + 3 = 8
-5 - 3 = -5 + (-3) = -8
-5 - (-3) = -5 + 3 = -2

Multiplication

Multiply the absolute values as usual, then determine the sign: same signs give a positive product; different signs give a negative product.

(+) × (+) = (+)    4 × 3 = 12
(-) × (-) = (+)    -4 × -3 = 12
(+) × (-) = (-)    4 × -3 = -12
(-) × (+) = (-)    -4 × 3 = -12

Division

The sign rules for division are identical to multiplication: same signs give a positive quotient; different signs give a negative quotient.

(+) ÷ (+) = (+)    12 ÷ 4 = 3
(-) ÷ (-) = (+)    -12 ÷ -4 = 3
(+) ÷ (-) = (-)    12 ÷ -4 = -3
(-) ÷ (+) = (-)    -12 ÷ 4 = -3

Quick Reference: Sign Rules for Multiplication & Division

  • Same signs → positive result (+ × + = +,   − × − = +)
  • Different signs → negative result (+ × − = −,   − × + = −)
  • This rule applies identically whether you're multiplying or dividing.
  • For addition/subtraction, signs don't multiply — think in terms of a number line instead (see below).
Figure 1.6: On a number line, adding a positive number moves right; adding a negative number (or subtracting a positive) moves left.
Tip: Treat every minus sign as "add the opposite." Two signs together always collapse to one: same signs → positive, different signs → negative (++ = +, -- = +, +- = -, -+ = -).

1.7 Metric Mass & Temperature Conversions

Metric Mass: Kilogram Down to Microgram

The metric system scales by powers of 10. Moving down the ladder (to a smaller unit) means multiplying; moving up the ladder (to a larger unit) means dividing.

1 kilogram (kg) = 1,000 grams (g)
1 gram (g) = 1,000 milligrams (mg)
1 milligram (mg) = 1,000 micrograms (mcg or µg)

1 kg = 1,000 g = 1,000,000 mg = 1,000,000,000 mcg
  • kg → g: multiply by 1,000 (e.g., 2.5 kg = 2,500 g)
  • g → mg: multiply by 1,000 (e.g., 3 g = 3,000 mg)
  • mg → mcg: multiply by 1,000 (e.g., 0.5 mg = 500 mcg)
  • Going the other direction (small → large), divide by 1,000 at each step (e.g., 750 mg ÷ 1,000 = 0.75 g)
Figure 1.7a: Metric mass ladder — multiply by 1,000 moving right (larger to smaller unit); divide by 1,000 moving left.

Celsius ↔ Fahrenheit Conversion

Celsius and Fahrenheit are two temperature scales with different zero points and different-sized degrees, so converting between them requires both a multiplication/division step and an addition/subtraction step.

°F = (°C × 9/5) + 32    or equivalently    °F = (°C × 1.8) + 32

°C = (°F - 32) × 5/9
  • C → F example: Convert 20°C to °F: (20 × 9/5) + 32 = 36 + 32 = 68°F
  • F → C example: Convert 68°F to °C: (68 - 32) × 5/9 = 36 × 5/9 = 20°C
  • Reference points: 0°C = 32°F (freezing); 100°C = 212°F (boiling); 37°C = 98.6°F (body temperature)
Figure 1.7b: Matching reference points on the Celsius and Fahrenheit scales.
Module 2 · Fractions, Decimals & Percentages

2.1 Fraction Types, Equivalent Fractions & Reducing

A fraction represents part of a whole, written as a numerator (parts you have) over a denominator (parts in one whole unit).

Types of Fractions

  • Proper fraction: numerator < denominator, value less than 1 (e.g., 3/4)
  • Improper fraction: numerator ≥ denominator, value 1 or greater (e.g., 7/4)
  • Mixed number: a whole number plus a proper fraction (e.g., 1 3/4)

Converting Improper Fractions ↔ Mixed Numbers

Improper → Mixed: divide numerator by denominator; quotient = whole number, remainder = new numerator
9/5 → 9 ÷ 5 = 1 remainder 4 → 1 4/5

Mixed → Improper: (whole × denominator) + numerator, over the same denominator
1 4/5 → (1 × 5 + 4)/5 = 9/5

Equivalent Fractions

Multiplying (or dividing) both the numerator and denominator by the same nonzero number produces an equivalent fraction — the value doesn't change.

2/5 = ?/15 → 15 ÷ 5 = 3, so multiply numerator by 3: 2/5 = 6/15

Reducing Fractions

A fraction is fully reduced when the numerator and denominator share no common factors. Divide both by their greatest common factor (or cancel shared prime factors) until none remain.

6/8 = (6÷2)/(8÷2) = 3/4
-28/42 = (-28÷7)/(42÷7) = -4/6 = -2/3

The Butterfly Method (Checking Equivalence)

To check if two fractions are equal, cross-multiply: numerator₁ × denominator₂ vs. numerator₂ × denominator₁. If the products match, the fractions are equivalent.

3/8 vs. 15/40: 3 × 40 = 120 and 8 × 15 = 120 → equal
Figure 2.1: 3/4 and 6/8 shade the same portion of the whole — equivalent fractions.

2.2 Least Common Denominator & Adding/Subtracting Fractions

Multiples & Least Common Multiple (LCM)

A multiple is what you get by multiplying a number by an integer. The least common multiple of two numbers is the smallest positive number both divide into evenly.

  • No shared factor: multiply the two numbers together (LCM of 2 and 3 = 6)
  • Shared factor: list multiples of the larger number until the smaller one divides evenly (LCM of 6 and 8 = 24)
  • One divides the other: the larger number is the LCM (LCM of 6 and 12 = 12)

Least Common Denominator (LCD)

To add or subtract fractions, they need the same denominator. The LCD is the LCM of the denominators. Convert each fraction to an equivalent fraction using the LCD before combining.

1/4 + 1/3: LCD = 12
1/4 = 3/12 and 1/3 = 4/12
3/12 + 4/12 = 7/12

Same-Denominator Rule

Add or subtract only the numerators; keep the denominator the same. Reduce if needed.

2/7 + 3/7 = 5/7
5/6 - 1/6 = 4/6 = 2/3

Adding/Subtracting Mixed Numbers

Convert mixed numbers to improper fractions first, find the LCD, combine, then convert the answer back to a mixed number and reduce.

6 2/3 + 7 3/4 → 20/3 + 31/4 = 80/12 + 93/12 = 173/12 = 14 5/12

2.3 Multiplying & Dividing Fractions

Multiplying Fractions

Unlike addition, multiplication does not require a common denominator. Multiply straight across: numerator × numerator, denominator × denominator.

3/5 × 1/5 = (3×1)/(5×5) = 3/25

Canceling Before Multiplying

If a numerator and a denominator (from either fraction) share a common factor, cancel it first to simplify the multiplication.

7/9 × 1/14 = 7/9 × 1/14 → cancel the 7s → 1/9 × 1/2 = 1/18

Multiplying Mixed Numbers

Convert to improper fractions first, multiply, then convert the result back to a mixed number and reduce.

6 2/3 × 1/2 = 20/3 × 1/2 = 20/6 = 3 1/3

Dividing Fractions

Dividing by a fraction means multiplying by its reciprocal (flip the numerator and denominator of the divisor).

1/2 ÷ 2/3 = 1/2 × 3/2 = 3/4

Dividing Mixed Numbers

Convert mixed numbers to improper fractions, change ÷ to ×, use the reciprocal of the second fraction, multiply, then simplify.

6 1/3 ÷ 1/4 = 19/3 × 4/1 = 76/3 = 25 1/3
Tip: "Keep, Change, Flip" for division: keep the first fraction, change ÷ to ×, flip (reciprocate) the second fraction. Then multiply straight across.

2.4 Decimals: Place Value, Rounding & Operations

Place Value

Each position to the right of the decimal point represents a smaller fraction of a whole: tenths, hundredths, thousandths, and so on. There is no "ones" place mirrored on the right — the first position after the decimal is tenths.

1.538 → 5 tenths, 3 hundredths, 8 thousandths → read as "one and five hundred thirty-eight thousandths"

Rounding Rules of Thumb

Identify the digit in the place you're rounding to. Look at the digit immediately to its right: if it's 4 or less, leave the rounding digit unchanged; if it's 5 or greater, add 1 to the rounding digit. Drop (or zero out) everything after.

138.791 rounded to the nearest tens → 140
138.791 rounded to the nearest hundredths → 138.79

Adding & Subtracting Decimals

Line up the decimal points vertically, fill in missing places with zeros as placeholders, then add or subtract as with whole numbers, bringing the decimal point straight down.

Multiplying Decimals

Multiply as if the numbers were whole numbers (no placeholder zeros needed), then count the total number of decimal places in both factors and place the decimal point that many spots from the right in the product.

Tenths (1 decimal place) × hundredths (2 decimal places) → product has 3 decimal places

Dividing Decimals

Move the decimal point in the divisor to the right until it becomes a whole number, moving the decimal point in the dividend the same number of places. Then divide as usual, placing the quotient's decimal point directly above the dividend's.

375 ÷ 0.25 → move 2 places → 37,500 ÷ 25 = 1,500

2.5 Percentages & Converting Fractions, Decimals & Percentages

What Percentages Represent

A percentage is a part out of 100, and is always relative to some whole quantity — it's never used alone (e.g., "50% off," not just "50%").

Adding & Subtracting Percentages

Add or subtract like decimals, keeping the % sign on the result.

42.5% + 16% = 58.5%
94% - 33.45% = 60.55%

Multiplying & Dividing Percentages

Convert the percentage to a decimal first (move the decimal point two places left, i.e., divide by 100), then multiply or divide as usual.

"75% of 5 mL" → 0.75 × 5 = 3.75 mL
"7% of 200" → 0.07 × 200 = 14

Converting Between Fractions, Decimals & Percentages

Fraction → Decimal: divide numerator by denominator (9/5 → 1.8)
Decimal → Percentage: multiply by 100, add % (0.1875 → 18.75%)
Percentage → Decimal: divide by 100 (86.25% → 0.8625)
Decimal → Fraction: put digits after the decimal over the matching place value, then reduce (0.50 → 50/100 → 1/2)
Percentage → Fraction: put the number over 100, then reduce (75% → 75/100 → 3/4)
Figure 2.5: Fractions, decimals, and percentages are three interchangeable representations of the same value.

2.6 Ratios, Proportions & Solving for an Unknown

Ratios & Rates

A ratio compares two quantities (e.g., 4 to 5). A rate is a ratio comparing quantities with different units (e.g., mg per hour).

Proportions

A proportion states that two ratios are equal. Use the Butterfly Method (cross-multiplication) to check whether two ratios are truly proportional.

4/5 = 20/25? → 4 × 25 = 100 and 5 × 20 = 100 → proportional

Solving a Conditional Proportion (Unknown Value)

When one value in a proportion is unknown, find the conversion number by dividing the two known values on the side with the missing piece, then apply it to the other side.

3/4 = y/12 → 12 ÷ 4 = 3 (conversion number) → y = 3 × 3 = 9

The Percent Proportion

Set the percentage as a ratio over 100, equal to the part over the whole, and solve using cross-multiplication.

"9% commission on $450,000" → 9/100 = x/450,000 → x = (9 × 450,000)/100 = $40,500
Tip: The Butterfly Method works for both checking equivalence and solving for an unknown in a proportion — cross-multiply the diagonals, then divide to isolate the variable.

2.7 Unit Conversions: Household, Metric & Unit Multipliers

The Unit Multiplier Method

List your starting units and desired units, find the conversion rate, then build a fraction (unit multiplier) positioned so the unwanted unit cancels — it must appear in the numerator of one fraction and the denominator of the other.

3 ft → in: 3 ft/1 × 12 in/1 ft = 36 in (ft cancels)

Common Household Volume Conversions

1 Tbsp = 3 tsp  |  1 fl oz = 2 Tbsp  |  1 cup = 8 fl oz
1 pint = 2 cups  |  1 quart = 2 pints  |  1 gallon = 4 quarts

Common Metric Conversions

1 L = 1,000 mL  |  1 kg = 1,000 g  |  1 g = 1,000 mg  |  1 mg = 1,000 mcg (µg)

Household ↔ Metric Conversions

1 cc = 1 mL  |  1 fl oz = 30 mL  |  1 L ≈ 1.057 qt
1 tsp = 5 mL  |  1 kg ≈ 2.2 lb  |  1 oz ≈ 28.35 g

Chaining Multiple Unit Multipliers

When no single conversion rate connects the starting and desired units, chain several unit multipliers together so units cancel in sequence.

5 gal → pt: 5 gal/1 × 4 qt/1 gal × 2 pt/1 qt = 40 pt
Figure 2.7: Chaining unit multipliers converts gallons to pints through an intermediate unit (quarts).
Module 3 · Basic Algebra

3.1 Algebraic Notation: Terms, Coefficients, Exponents & Degree

Constants, Variables & Operations

A constant is a number with a fixed value (0, 1.5, -10, π). A variable is a symbol (usually a letter) that holds the place of a numerical value. An operation is a procedure — like addition or multiplication — that generates a new value.

Coefficients & Terms

A coefficient is the number multiplying a variable (in 16x, 16 is the coefficient). A variable written alone has an invisible coefficient of 1. A term is a constant, a variable, or constants and variables multiplied together (e.g., 15a²b). Writing a variable and a number next to each other with no symbol always means multiplication (2x means 2 × x).

Degree

The degree of a term or expression is its highest exponent. A lone constant has degree 0 (12 = 12x⁰); a variable with no visible exponent has degree 1.

3x² → degree 2
7a → degree 1 (7a = 7a¹)
9m⁴ - 2z² → degree 4 (the highest exponent among all terms)
12 → degree 0

Algebraic Expressions

An algebraic expression is a collection of terms connected by + or - signs. By convention, expressions are written with the highest-degree term first, descending to constants last (e.g., y³ - 4x - 51).

Figure 3.1: Anatomy of the term 15a²b — coefficient 15, variables a and b, exponent 2 on a.

3.2 Substitution & Combining Like Terms

The Substitution Method

To evaluate an expression when a variable's value is known, replace the variable with its value (parentheses help keep track), then simplify using order of operations.

If x = -3 and y = 6, evaluate (x + y)² + y:
(-3 + 6)² + 6 = (3)² + 6 = 9 + 6 = 15

Identifying Like Terms

Like terms have identical variables raised to identical exponents — coefficients don't matter for this check. Terms like 7x and 7y are not like terms (different variables); 3m and 7m² are not like terms (different exponents).

Like: 3D² and 6D² (same variable, same exponent)
Not like: 7x and 7y (different variables)
Not like: 3m and 7m² (different exponents)

Combining Like Terms

Add or subtract the coefficients of like terms, keeping the variable part unchanged. Unlike terms are simply written side by side, typically in descending degree order.

4h + 7h = 11h
10d² - 8d + 5e - 3d = 10d² - 11d + 5e

3.3 The Distributive Property

The distributive property multiplies a single term by each term inside a set of parentheses.

A(B + C) = AB + AC
A(B - C) = AB - AC

Worked Examples

2(y + 3) = 2y + 6
5x(3y + 10) = 15xy + 50x
3x(5y - 2) = 15xy - 6x
-6a(2a - 4) = -12a² - (-24a) = -12a² + 24a

After distributing, check whether the resulting terms are like terms — if not, they stay as a two-term expression.

Figure 3.3: A is distributed to both B and C inside the parentheses.

3.4 Solving Linear Equations

An equation is like a balanced scale — whatever operation you perform on one side, you must perform on the other to keep it balanced.

Principles of Equality

  • Addition/Subtraction Principle: adding or subtracting the same number from both sides preserves equality.
  • Multiplication/Division Principle: multiplying or dividing both sides by the same nonzero number preserves equality.
x - 2 = 12 → +2 both sides → x = 14
2x = 10 → ÷2 both sides → x = 5
-x = 6 → ÷(-1) both sides → x = -6

Solving Longer (Multi-Step) Equations

  1. Simplify each side (distribute, combine like terms)
  2. Move variable terms to one side, constants to the other (addition/subtraction principle)
  3. Simplify again if needed
  4. Isolate the variable (multiplication/division principle)
  5. Check by substituting the solution back into the original equation
3(x - 1) = -1 + 3x - 2x + 8
3x - 3 = x + 7 → 2x - 3 = 7 → 2x = 10 → x = 5

Cross-Multiplication (Butterfly Method) for Equations

For equations of the form a/b = c/d, cross-multiply the "wings," then divide to isolate the variable — this also solves equations with fraction coefficients like (3/4)x = 1/2.

x/24 = 1/6 → 6x = 24 → x = 4
(3/4)x = 1/2 → multiply both sides by 4/3 → x = 2/3
Tip: Whatever you do to one side, do to the other. Undo addition/subtraction first, then multiplication/division — you're "unwrapping" the variable in reverse order of operations.

3.5 The Coordinate Plane & Plotting Points

The coordinate plane is formed by a horizontal x-axis and a vertical y-axis, meeting at the origin (0, 0). A point is written as an ordered pair (x, y): move right/left along the x-axis first, then up/down for the y-value.

Quadrant I = (+, +)  |  Quadrant II = (-, +)
Quadrant III = (-, -)  |  Quadrant IV = (+, -)
Figure 3.5: The four quadrants, with example points A(3, -3) in Quadrant IV and B(-2, 1) in Quadrant II.

3.6 Slope of a Line

Slope describes a line's steepness and direction — the ratio of vertical change (rise) to horizontal change (run).

slope = rise/run = (y₂ - y₁)/(x₂ - x₁)

Four Types of Slope

  • Positive: line rises left to right
  • Negative: line falls left to right
  • Zero: perfectly horizontal line
  • Undefined: perfectly vertical line
Figure 3.6: The four slope types — positive, negative, zero, and undefined.

Calculating Slope from Two Points

Points (1, 5) and (0, -3):
slope = (-3 - 5)/(0 - 1) = -8/-1 = 8
Tip: Remember slope as "rise over run" — and keep the x's and y's in the same order in the formula: (y₂ - y₁)/(x₂ - x₁). Swapping only one pair flips the sign!

3.7 Slope-Intercept Form & Graphing Linear Equations

A linear equation (degree 1, no exponents greater than 1) graphs as a straight line. Slope-intercept form isolates y to reveal the slope and y-intercept at a glance.

y = mx + b
m = slope  |  b = y-intercept, the point (0, b)

Converting to Slope-Intercept Form

Move the y term to the left, everything else to the right, then divide so y's coefficient is 1.

x + 8 = 2y → y = (1/2)x + 4 → m = 1/2, b = 4

Graphing Steps

  1. Write the equation in y = mx + b form
  2. Plot the y-intercept (0, b)
  3. Use m as rise/run to plot a second point
  4. Draw a straight line through the points
Figure 3.7: Graphing y = 3x + 2 by starting at the y-intercept (0, 2) and using slope 3/1 to find the next point.

3.8 Linear Inequalities

Solving One-Variable Inequalities

Solve exactly like an equation, with one critical rule: flip the inequality sign whenever you multiply or divide both sides by a negative number.

-x/2 ≥ 1 → multiply by -2 (flip sign) → x ≤ -2

Graphing on a Number Line

Open circle = value excluded (<, >). Closed circle = value included (≤, ≥). Shade the direction that satisfies the inequality.

-2 < x ≤ 1 → open circle at -2, closed circle at 1, shaded between

Graphing Two-Variable Inequalities

  1. Write in y = mx + b form (keeping the inequality symbol)
  2. Plot the y-intercept and use slope to find a second point
  3. Draw a dashed line for < or >; a solid line for ≤ or ≥
  4. Test a point not on the line — if it satisfies the inequality, shade that side; if not, shade the other side
Shortcut: y < mx + b or y ≤ mx + b → shade below the line
y > mx + b or y ≥ mx + b → shade above the line
Figure 3.8: A dashed boundary line (strict inequality) with the solution region shaded above it.
Module 4 · Descriptive Statistics for a Single Variable

4.1 Types of Data: Quantitative vs. Categorical

Quantitative (numerical) data consists of values that can be counted or measured (e.g., number of employees, average salary). Categorical (qualitative) data consists of names, labels, or groups that are not measured as quantities (e.g., industry type, job title).

Note: numbers can still be categorical if they only label something rather than measure it — a basketball player's jersey number or a zip code doesn't represent a quantity, so both are categorical.

Quantitative: height, salary, number of visits
Categorical: blood type, operating system, degree program

4.2 Graphical Displays for Categorical Data

Pie Charts

Pie charts show categorical data as slices of a circle, where each slice's size reflects its share of the whole (all slices should sum to about 100%). They work best with a small number of categories with clearly different sizes — with many similarly-sized slices, differences become hard to read.

Bar Charts

Bar charts display counts or frequencies for each category as separate bars. They make it easy to compare categories directly, even when differences are small.

Pie Chart → parts of a whole (e.g., % of customers by education level)
Bar Chart → counts/frequencies by category (e.g., sales by month)
Figure 4.2: Pie charts show proportion of a whole; bar charts compare counts across categories.

4.3 Graphical Displays for Quantitative Data

Dot Plots

Each data value is shown as a dot along a horizontal axis, with repeated values stacked vertically. Best for small data sets — useful for spotting clusters, gaps, and outliers.

Stem-and-Leaf Plots

Each value is split into a stem (leading digit(s)) and a leaf (last digit). Stems with no leaves are still listed, to reveal gaps.

Value 36 → stem 3, leaf 6
Stems: 0,1,2,3,4,5 | Leaves for stem 3: 1,2,2,2,2,3,6

Histograms

A histogram groups quantitative data into intervals and shows the count (frequency) in each as a bar. Unlike a bar chart (categorical, gaps between bars), histogram bars touch, reflecting continuous numeric intervals.

Histogram → quantitative data by interval
Bar Chart → categorical data by category

Suggested number of intervals: 5–7 for under 50 data points, 6–10 for 50–100, 7–12 for 100–250, 10–20 for over 250.

4.4 Describing Distribution Shape

When describing a distribution, address its shape, spread, center, and any possible outliers.

Common Shapes

  • Symmetric (bell-shaped): left and right halves mirror each other; most frequent value(s) in the middle
  • Skewed right (positively skewed): long tail extends to the right; mean is pulled higher than the median
  • Skewed left (negatively skewed): long tail extends to the left; mean is pulled lower than the median
  • Uniform: roughly equal frequency across the whole range
  • U-shaped: symmetric with a "valley" instead of a peak
  • Bimodal / multimodal: two (or more) distinct peaks
Figure 4.4: Six common distribution shapes.

Because the mean is pulled toward the tail in a skewed distribution, median and IQR are the preferred measures of center and spread for skewed data; mean and standard deviation are preferred for symmetric data.

4.5 Measures of Center & Spread

Descriptive statistics summarize and organize data. A population is the entire group being studied; a sample is a subset drawn from it. Numbers that describe a population are parameters; numbers that describe a sample are statistics.

Measures of Center

The mean is the arithmetic average and is sensitive to outliers. The median is the middle value when data is ordered and resists outliers. The mode is the most frequent value.

Mean (x̄) = Σx / n

Measures of Spread

The range is the max minus the min. Variance and standard deviation measure how far data points typically fall from the mean. The interquartile range (IQR) measures the spread of the middle 50% of data and is used to detect outliers.

Sample Variance (s²) = Σ(x - x̄)² / (n - 1)
Sample Standard Deviation (s) = √s²
IQR = Q3 - Q1
Figure 4.5: In a right-skewed distribution, the tail pulls the mean to the right of the median, while the mode marks the peak.

4.6 The Empirical Rule (68-95-99.7) for Normal Distributions

For a bell-shaped (normal) distribution, the Empirical Rule (Standard Deviation Rule) describes how data clusters around the mean.

≈68% of values fall within 1 standard deviation of the mean
≈95% of values fall within 2 standard deviations of the mean
≈99.7% of values fall within 3 standard deviations of the mean
Figure 4.6: In a normal distribution, roughly 68% of values lie within 1 SD of the mean, 95% within 2 SD.

Worked Example

Mean build time = 280 days, SD = 13 days. 68% of builds finish between 280 - 13 = 267 and 280 + 13 = 293 days. Since 254 and 306 are each 2 SD from the mean (280 ± 26), 95% of builds fall in that range, leaving 2.5% taking longer than 306 days (half of the remaining 5%, since the curve is symmetric).

Tip: Split the 95%/68% figures in half around the mean: 47.5% falls between the mean and +2 SD, and 34% between the mean and +1 SD — handy for "between two values" questions.

4.7 Five-Number Summary, Box Plots & the 1.5×IQR Outlier Rule

Five-Number Summary

Minimum, first quartile (Q1), median (Q2), third quartile (Q3), and maximum. Each quarter of a box plot represents 25% of the data.

Box Plot Anatomy

A box plot has two whiskers (each 25% of the data) and a box divided by the median into two halves (each also 25%). The box spans from Q1 to Q3.

Figure 4.7: Box plot anatomy — each whisker and each box half represents 25% of the data.

The 1.5×IQR Rule for Outliers

A value is a potential outlier if it falls more than 1.5×IQR below Q1 or above Q3.

Lower bound = Q1 - 1.5 × IQR
Upper bound = Q3 + 1.5 × IQR
Example: Q1 = 2.5, Q3 = 8, IQR = 5.5
Bounds: 2.5 - 8.25 = -5.75 and 8 + 8.25 = 16.25

4.8 Misrepresenting Data with Graphical Displays

Graphs can distort data in several common ways — always check the axes, labels, and dimensionality before drawing conclusions.

  • Truncated axes: starting the vertical axis above zero exaggerates differences between bars
  • Missing labels or units: a pie chart without percentages makes it hard to judge relative sizes
  • 2D/3D figures for 1D data: using area or volume (circles, cylinders) to represent a single measurement distorts the true proportional difference, since the eye perceives area/volume rather than the underlying linear value

Always ask: does the visual impression match what the actual numbers show?

Module 5 · Descriptive Statistics for Two Variables

5.1 Explanatory & Response Variables

When studying two variables, the explanatory variable (independent variable) is the suspected cause; the response variable (dependent variable) is the suspected effect. In an experiment, researchers manipulate the explanatory variable's values, called treatments, and measure the resulting change in the response variable on each experimental unit (the individual or object being measured).

Explanatory (cause) → Response (effect)
e.g., Temperature (explanatory) → Employee absenteeism (response)

Memory tip: "explanatory" contains an "x" — the explanatory variable's values go on the x-axis of a scatterplot.

5.2 Role-Type Classification: C→C, C→Q, Q→Q

The right graphical display and numerical analysis for two-variable data depends on whether each variable is categorical (C) or quantitative (Q).

C → C (both categorical): two-way (contingency) table
C → Q (categorical explanatory, quantitative response): side-by-side box plots
Q → Q (both quantitative): scatterplot
Figure 5.2: Matching data type combinations to the correct graphical display and numerical measure.

5.3 Two-Way Tables & Conditional Percentages

A two-way (contingency) table shows the relationship between two categorical variables — rows for one variable's categories, columns for the other's.

  • Joint frequency: the count in a single interior cell (a specific row-and-column combination)
  • Marginal frequency: a row or column total, found in the "margins" of the table
  • Conditional percentage: a cell's count divided by its row (or column) total — used to see how the response varies across levels of the explanatory variable

Calculate conditional percentages across whichever dimension (row or column) holds the explanatory variable — each row (or column) of percentages should sum to 100%.

Low stress: 667 early retirements / 3,338 total = 20%
High stress: 214 early retirements / 2,676 total = 8%
→ Low-stress executives retire early at more than double the rate of high-stress executives

5.4 Scatterplots & the Correlation Coefficient (r)

A scatterplot displays paired quantitative data, always with the explanatory variable on the x-axis and the response variable on the y-axis. Describe a scatterplot using three characteristics:

  • Direction: positive (points trend up and to the right) or negative (points trend down and to the right)
  • Form: linear (roughly a straight line) or nonlinear/curvilinear (curved pattern)
  • Strength: strong (tightly clustered around a line) vs. weak/none (loosely scattered)
Figure 5.4a: Four scatterplot patterns — positive, negative, no correlation, and nonlinear (curvilinear).

The Correlation Coefficient (r)

The correlation coefficient r quantifies the strength and direction of a linear relationship, ranging from -1 to 1.

r near -1 → strong negative linear correlation
r near 0 → weak or no linear correlation
r near +1 → strong positive linear correlation

Note: r only describes linear relationships — a strong curvilinear pattern can have a low r value even though the variables are clearly related.

1P. Interpreting the Strength of the Correlation Coefficient (r)

While there's no universal cutoff, a common guideline classifies |r| into three bands:

Weak: -0.3 < r < 0.3
Moderate: -0.7 < r ≤ -0.3 or 0.3 ≤ r < 0.7
Strong: -1 ≤ r ≤ -0.7 or 0.7 ≤ r ≤ 1
Figure 5.4b: A common (not universal) scale for classifying correlation strength by |r|.

5.5 The Effect of Outliers on Two-Variable Analysis

A scatterplot outlier is a point that clearly doesn't fit the overall trend of the rest of the data (distinct from a cluster, which is a group of points set apart from the majority but consistent with each other).

  • An outlier far from the line of best fit weakens the correlation, pulling r closer to 0
  • An outlier near the line of best fit has little effect, and may even slightly strengthen the correlation
  • Outliers have a larger effect on smaller data sets — the same outlier can swing correlation from weak to very strong once removed in a small sample

Always plot the data first — a single outlier can dramatically change the apparent strength of a relationship, so it's important to identify it before interpreting the correlation coefficient.

Module 6 · Correlation & Regression

6.1 Sampling Methods & Study Design

A sampling frame is the list of everyone/everything eligible to be included in a study; the sampling method is the technique used to choose the actual sample from that frame.

Common Sampling Methods

  • Voluntary sample: everyone in the frame is invited; whoever responds becomes the sample. Easy to run, but prone to bias (non-representative volunteers).
  • Stratified sample: the population is split into distinct categories (strata), and a set number of responses is drawn from each — good when the population naturally divides into groups.
  • Cluster sample: entire pre-existing groups (clusters) are randomly selected and every member of each chosen cluster is included — practical for very large, geographically spread populations.

Observational vs. Experimental Studies

An observational study simply records what happens without assigning any treatment. An experimental study assigns a treatment to one group and compares it to a control group (which may receive a placebo, an inactive treatment used as a baseline). Blinding keeps subjects unaware of which group they're in, preventing the placebo effect from biasing results.

Only an experimental study (with treatment + control groups) can establish causation.
An observational study can only show association.

Bias occurs when the data-collection method skews the results — often from a mismatch between the research question, the population, the sampling frame, or the sampling method (e.g., a voluntary sample skewing toward a particular demographic).

6.2 Association, Causation & Lurking Variables

Association means two variables show a relationship or pattern (visible on a scatterplot). Causation means one variable's change directly produces the change in the other — this can only be established through a properly designed experiment, never through observation alone.

Association: "these two variables move together"
Causation: "changing one variable directly changes the other" — requires an experiment

Lurking Variables

A lurking variable is a variable not included in the study that influences both variables being examined, creating an association between them that isn't a direct cause-and-effect relationship.

Classic example: ice cream sales and crime rates are associated, but temperature (a lurking variable) drives both — ice cream doesn't cause crime.

Never assume causation just because two variables are associated — always consider whether a lurking variable could explain the pattern.

6.3 Simpson’s Paradox

Simpson's Paradox occurs when a trend appears consistently within separate groups of data but reverses (or disappears) once the groups are combined.

Version B beats Version A in every individual trial...
...but combining all trials shows Version A performing better overall.

This happens because of a lurking variable — typically an uneven distribution of sample sizes across the groups being combined (e.g., one version being tested mostly in easy trials, the other mostly in hard trials). Simpson's Paradox is avoided when group sizes are balanced across all subgroups being compared.

Figure 6.3: Version B outperforms in both individual trials, yet Version A wins overall once the trials are combined.

6.4 The Linear Regression Equation & Least Squares

Simple linear regression predicts a response variable (y) from an explanatory variable (x) using a straight-line model in slope-intercept form.

ŷ = mx + b
m = slope: predicted change in y for each 1-unit increase in x
b = y-intercept: predicted y-value when x = 0

The least squares (ordinary least squares/OLS) regression line is the line that minimizes the sum of the squared vertical distances between the data points and the line — this is the standard "line of best fit."

Example: ŷ = 10.33x - 2330 (advertising $ → projected sales $)
Slope 10.33 → each additional $1 spent on ads predicts $10.33 more in sales
Figure 6.4: The least squares line minimizes the total squared vertical distance from each point to the line.
Tip: "Explanatory has an x in it" — always plot the explanatory variable on the x-axis. In ŷ = mx + b, m tells you the predicted change in y per 1-unit increase in x; b is the prediction when x = 0.

6.5 Interpolation, Extrapolation & Problems in Regression Analysis

Interpolation vs. Extrapolation

Interpolation predicts a value within the range of the observed data — generally reliable. Extrapolation predicts a value outside that range — less reliable the farther it goes, since the linear pattern may not continue to hold.

Data observed for ages 4-14: predicting height at age 8 = interpolation (reliable)
Using the same line to predict height at age 50 = extrapolation (unreliable — growth doesn't continue linearly into adulthood)

Common Pitfalls in Regression Analysis

  • Inappropriate extrapolation: applying the regression line far outside the studied range
  • Assuming causation: a strong regression relationship still only shows association unless from a true experiment
  • Non-representative sample: missing data or excluded population segments can bias the regression, especially if the missing data is non-random
  • Small sample size: smaller samples are less reliable and more sensitive to outliers or random variation
Tip: Interpolation = inside the data (safe). Extrapolation = outside the data (risky) — the farther out, the shakier the prediction.
Module 7 · Probability

7.1 Probability Basics: Rules, Notation & Theoretical vs. Empirical

Probability describes the likelihood of an event, expressed as a value between 0 (impossible) and 1 (certain). The sample space is the set of all possible outcomes. Events are mutually exclusive if they cannot both happen, and independent if one occurring does not affect the probability of the other.

  • Addition Rule: used to find the probability that A or B occurs.
  • Multiplication Rule: used to find the probability that A and B both occur.
  • Complement Rule: the probability an event does not occur.
  • Conditional Probability: the probability of A given that B has already occurred.
P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A and B)
P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B | A)
P(A') = 1 - P(A)
P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B)
Figure 7.1: Venn diagram of events A and B within sample space S. The overlap represents P(A and B).

2A. Theoretical vs. Empirical Probability & the Law of Large Numbers

Theoretical (classical) probability applies when all outcomes are equally likely: divide the number of favorable outcomes by the total number of possible outcomes.

P(event) = (number of favorable outcomes) / (total possible outcomes)
e.g., 3 blue suits out of 5 total → P(blue) = 3/5 = 60%

Empirical (observational) probability estimates probability from actual trial results using relative frequency — useful when outcomes aren't equally likely (e.g., predicting which sports team wins).

Relative frequency = (times event occurred) / (total trials)

The Law of Large Numbers

As the number of trials increases, relative frequency converges toward the true theoretical probability. Small samples can vary widely from the expected value; large samples settle in much closer.

10 coin flips → relative frequency of heads might range widely (e.g., 0.3-0.7)
1,000 coin flips → relative frequency settles close to 0.5

7.2 Sets, Venn Diagram Vocabulary & Disjoint Events

A set is a collection of unique elements. Set A is a subset of set B if every element of A is also in B. The empty set {} has no elements.

Venn Diagram Vocabulary

  • Universe (sample space): everything under consideration, including outcomes outside any defined event
  • Not A (complement of A): everything in the universe that is not in A
  • A and B (intersection): outcomes in both A and B — always the same size or smaller than either set alone
  • A or B (union): outcomes in A, B, or both — usually larger than either set alone
E = {0, 10, 100}, F = {-2, -1, 0, 1, 2} → E and F (intersection) = {0}
C = {2, 4, 6}, D = {1, 3, 5} → C or D (union) = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}

Disjoint Sets/Events

Two events are disjoint if they share no outcomes — they cannot both occur, so P(A and B) = 0 for disjoint events.

7.3 Independent vs. Dependent Events & Tests for Independence

Events are independent if one occurring doesn't change the probability of the other (e.g., two separate coin flips). Events are dependent if one occurring does affect the other's probability (e.g., drawing cards without replacement).

Four Equivalent Tests for Independence

If any one of these holds, the events are independent (choose whichever is easiest given your data):

1. P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)
2. P(A|B) = P(A)
3. P(B|A) = P(B)
4. P(B|A) = P(B|not A)

Disjoint events are always dependent — knowing one occurred tells you the other definitely did not.

7.4 The General Multiplication Rule & Conditional Probability

The notation P(B|A) reads "the probability of B, given A has occurred." Watch for the words given, of, and if to signal a conditional probability, and note that the "given" event isn't always stated first.

General Multiplication Rule: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B|A)
Rearranged → Conditional Probability Rule: P(B|A) = P(A and B) / P(A)

For independent events, P(B|A) = P(B), simplifying the rule to the Multiplication Rule for Independent Events:

P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) (independent events only)
For n identical independent events: P(all occur) = P(event)ⁿ
Example: P(rolling heads then a 4) = 1/2 × 1/6 = 1/12
Example: P(4 coin flips all heads) = (1/2)⁴ = 1/16
Tip: Watch for "given," "of," and "if" — they all signal a conditional probability P(B|A), and the "given" event isn't always stated first in the sentence.

7.5 The Addition Rule & Complement Rule

General Addition Rule

To avoid double-counting the overlap, subtract the intersection when combining two events with "or."

P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A and B)

For disjoint events (no overlap), this simplifies since P(A and B) = 0:

P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) (disjoint events only)

For independent events, substitute P(A) × P(B) for the intersection term:

P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A) × P(B) (independent events only)

Complement Rule

The complement of an event (not A) is everything else in the universe; complementary probabilities always sum to 1.

P(not A) = 1 - P(A)

This is especially useful for "at least one" problems, since "at least one X" and "no X" are complementary:

P(at least one X) = 1 - P(no X)
Example: 5 independent events each with 70% rejection chance →
P(at least one acceptance) = 1 - (0.7)⁵ = 1 - 0.168 = 0.832

Note: disjoint events are not necessarily complementary — complementary events must also cover the entire universe with no other possibilities.

7.6 Frequency Tables for Probability Analysis

A frequency (two-way) table organizes counts for two categorical variables — rows for one variable, columns for the other — making it easy to calculate probabilities directly from raw counts.

P(single category) = category total / grand total
P(A and B) = joint cell count / grand total
P(A|B) = joint cell count / B's row or column total

Frequency tables are also useful for testing independence empirically: compare conditional relative frequencies (e.g., P(B|A) vs. P(B|not A)) — if they're close, the variables behave as if independent.

7.7 Sample Spaces, Probability Trees & Problem-Solving Strategy

The sample space is the complete list of possible outcomes; the sample size is how many outcomes it contains. Three common ways to build a sample space:

  • A list: write out every outcome directly (risk of missing one without a systematic pattern)
  • A table: works well for exactly two variables — rows for one, columns for the other
  • A tree diagram: branches out each successive choice, and is the most reliable method for three or more sequential decisions
Two dice rolled: sample space has 36 outcomes (6 × 6)
P(sum of 7) = 6/36 = 1/6 (six combinations sum to 7)
Figure 7.7: A probability tree branches out each sequential choice to build the full sample space.

General Problem-Solving Strategy

  1. Identify how many events are involved and name them (A, B, ...)
  2. One event → theoretical probability or complement rule
  3. Two events → determine the connection: "and" (multiplication rule), "or" (addition rule), or "given" (conditional probability) — checking independence/disjointness as needed
  4. Three or more events → multiply probabilities if independent, or build a probability tree
Tip: When stuck, ask: how many events? One → theoretical probability or complement. Two → is it "and," "or," or "given"? Three-plus independent events → multiply straight across, or build a tree.
